    Comparing agents¶

    Is one agent better than another, and how many seeds does it take to say so? ajax.compare answers with AdaStop (Mathieu et al., "AdaStop: adaptive statistical testing for sound comparisons of Deep RL agents", TMLR 2024, arXiv:2306.10882). It trains a few seeds of each agent, tests, and trains more only while the answer is unclear.

    import ajax
from ajax.compare import compare, last_third

result = compare(
    {"SAC": lambda: ajax.SAC("Pendulum-v1"), "TD3": lambda: ajax.TD3("Pendulum-v1")},
    n_timesteps=20_000,
    eval_every=1_000,
    score=last_third,
)
result.decisions  # {("SAC", "TD3"): "equal"}, say
result.seeds  # the seeds each agent trained
result.plot()  # each agent's scores, the decisions as the title

    At each of up to K interims, every agent still in an undecided comparison trains n new seeds in one train call, so all of them run in one compiled program. Every pair of agents is compared, or with baseline="SAC" only SAC against each other agent. AdaStop itself takes scores from anywhere, one interim at a time, for runs recorded elsewhere.

    What it decides¶

    • "larger" or "smaller": the two agents' scores do not come from the same distribution, and the first agent's mean is larger (or smaller).
    • "equal": no difference was shown after n * K runs each. This does not show that the agents are equal: a small difference can need more runs than that.

    The chance of reporting any difference between agents that do not differ (the family-wise error) is at most alpha, whatever the number of comparisons. That holds exactly for two agents. With three or more it rests on the paper's shared permutations, which are an approximation there: over 20,000 simulated comparisons of equal agents at alpha = 0.05, two agents gave 0.044 and three or four gave 0.054 to 0.056.

    What a verdict leaves open¶

    Two numbers say how much a verdict tells, and belong next to it:

    from ajax.compare import difference, resolution

a, b = result.scores["SAC"], result.scores["TD3"]
gap, (low, high) = difference(a, b)  # mean(a) - mean(b), 95% bootstrap interval
smallest = resolution(a, b, n=5, K=5)  # the gap detected 80% of the time

    difference estimates the gap between the means. resolution is the smallest gap that AdaStop, with the same n, K and alpha, detects with probability power (0.8 by default) for scores spread like these. It simulates, as the paper's power study does, but resamples the observed scores instead of assuming a distribution. An "equal" rules out gaps much larger than the resolution; it says nothing about smaller ones. On normal scores with 32 runs each (n = 8, K = 4) the resolution is about 0.75 standard deviations.

    The score¶

    The test compares one number per run, and the choice of that number changes the question:

    • final_return: the last evaluation, or how the run ends.
    • last_third: the mean over the last third of the evaluations, or how steadily it stays there.
    • auc: the mean over all evaluations, or how fast it learns.

    They can disagree on the same runs. Choose the score before seeing the results: picking it afterwards voids the error guarantee.

    Cost¶

    At most n * K runs per agent: 25 with the defaults n = K = 5. A comparison stops early only on a clear difference; agents that differ little, or not at all, use every run. The paper advises n * K >= 30 for the means to be well estimated. Very few runs cannot reject anything: with n = 2 and K = 2 no outcome is extreme enough at alpha = 0.05. The test itself takes milliseconds; the training is the cost.

    The reference comparison reports an AdaStop verdict on its recorded runs.

    Differences from the paper and the authors' package¶

    The authors' adastop package (0.1.3) compares the observed difference with quantiles of the signed permuted differences, which about doubles the error rate (0.097 at alpha = 0.05 over 1,000 simulations of equal agents). It can also reject on rounding alone, when every permutation gives the same statistic. ajax.compare follows the paper instead, with these choices:

    • Absolute values on both sides of the test, as in the paper's Eqs. 8-9.
    • Statistics equal up to rounding tie.
    • Level that ties leave unspent at an interim carries to the next, as in the authors' package.
    • Permutations are always Monte Carlo (B draws, the identity first); the paper enumerates them when there are fewer than B.
    • No early acceptance (the paper's Appendix F, which gives no error bound for it), as in the package's default.
    • Every agent adds the same n runs per interim, as in the paper.

    One agent: how sure is a number?¶

    With nothing to compare, the question is how well one agent does: its success rate, say. estimate trains n seeds per train call until each chosen metric is known well enough, or max_seeds have trained:

    from ajax.compare import estimate, final_return, half_width, threshold


def final(evals):  # evals: train's evaluations, each (seeds, evaluations)
    return final_return(evals["Eval/episodic mean reward"])


result = estimate(
    lambda: ajax.PPO("CartPole-v1"),
    n_timesteps=100_000,
    eval_every=10_000,
    metrics={  # name: (per-seed score, its true bounds, when to stop)
        "solved": (lambda evals: final(evals) >= 475, (0, 1), threshold(0.8)),
        "final return": (final, (0, 500), half_width(25)),
    },
    n=16,
    max_seeds=128,
)
result.interval  # {"solved": (0.83, 1.0), "final return": (452.1, 500.0)}, say
result.mean, result.met, result.seeds, result.scores

    threshold(t) stops once the interval lies above or below t, deciding whether the mean is above t; half_width(w) once it is at most 2 * w wide. The intervals are the hedged betting confidence sequence of Waudby-Smith and Ramdas ("Estimating means of bounded random variables by betting", JRSS-B, Section 4.4), the authors' Bounded_Confseq from the adastop package: install the compare extra (uv sync --extra compare, or depend on ajax-rl[compare]). It comes from adastop's main branch until a release includes it.

    • Valid at every seed count at once: with probability at least 1 - delta the interval holds the true mean after every round, not only after a number of seeds fixed in advance, so stopping once the scores look good enough keeps the guarantee.
    • All metrics together: delta is split evenly between them (a union bound), so each extra metric widens every interval a little.
    • Only within true bounds: (low, high) must bound every score a run could get, not only those seen: (0, 500) for CartPole's return, about (-3254, 0) for Pendulum's. A score outside them is refused.
    • mean is the sample mean; the interval need not be centred on it.

    The cost is many seeds. A metric that is 1 on every seed has a 95% lower bound of 0.63 after 16 seeds and 0.92 after 64. On the reference comparison's recorded runs, 16 seeds left PPO on CartPole a success rate (final return of at least 475) of 0.88 within [0.45, 1] and a final return of 468 within [275, 500], and SAC on Pendulum a final return of -146 within [-1359, 0]. So decide against a threshold rather than aim for a width; prefer metrics whose bounds are close to what runs reach, such as success rates or KPI fractions, to returns; and train many seeds per round, which share one compiled program.
